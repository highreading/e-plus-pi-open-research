1. Arithmetic coverage and common denominator.
Let q_n=den(−X_n/D_n)>0. The preserved endpoint identification gives the same X_n,D_n at the same index in every local statement. Changing the sign of the ratio or multiplying the full polynomial triple by a common nonzero rational scalar leaves q_n unchanged. Thus the local bounds can be combined without estimating polynomial coefficient content.

For n≥max(14,N_D), the ternary residues 0,1,2 give respectively 2r_3, at least 2r_3+1, and 2r_3, where r_p=v_p(n!). The five-adic residues 0,1,2 give 2r_5; residue 3 gives at least 2r_5+1 conditional on D_n≠0. H4 and the published residue-four cancellation identity give 2r_5−1 on residue 4. At n≥14, r_5≥2, so removing the maximum with zero is legitimate. All residue classes are covered simultaneously. No CRT existence assumption or intersection-density argument is needed: each individual n has one ternary and one five-adic residue.

Both exponents 2r_3 and 2r_5−1 are nonnegative integers. Unique prime factorization therefore gives 3^(2r_3)5^(2r_5−1) dividing this same q_n, rather than merely two lower bounds whose product might otherwise be unjustified.

The threshold is eventual, not an effective assertion for all n≥14: N_D remains unspecified. The exceptional n=9 is explicitly excluded from H4 and does not affect any eventual conclusion.

2. Digit inequalities independently checked.
The candidate's elementary digit-count estimates imply 3^s_3(n)≤9n² and 5^(s_5(n)/2)≤25n². Legendre's formula consequently gives exactly its bound q_n≥(3√5)^n/(1125n^4).

The sharper inequality in main/note_000194.md is also valid. For integer base p≥2 and digit sum s=(p−1)k+r, 0≤r<p−1, the smallest integer having sum s is (r+1)p^k−1. To prove this minimization, if a higher digit is positive while a lower digit is less than p−1, move one unit of digit value from the higher position to the lower position. Digit sum is preserved and the integer strictly decreases. Repeating terminates with all lowest k digits equal to p−1, followed by r and then zeros. Therefore n+1≥(r+1)p^k.

For 0≤r≤p−1, concavity of log between 1 and p gives log(r+1)≥r log(p)/(p−1). Hence p^(s_p(n)/(p−1))≤n+1. Squaring gives both 3^s_3(n)≤(n+1)² and 5^(s_5(n)/2)≤(n+1)², yielding q_n≥(3√5)^n/[5(n+1)^4]. The weaker immutable candidate is correct without importing this refinement.

3. Error transfer and scope.
Assuming the cited transfer theorem applies to the identified matched ratio, the displayed positive asymptotic constant 4π/ρ³ permits an eventual lower bound with half that constant, independently of q_n's growth. Multiplication by the candidate's denominator bound yields exactly 2π/(1125ρ³) times n^(−4)(3√5/ρ²)^n. The exponential base exceeds one because 3√5>6>3+2√2=ρ². Consequently the absolute forms diverge along n→∞ and along every subsequence with indices tending to infinity. A merely unbounded sequence of indices that repeatedly returns to small indices need not itself tend to infinity; the intended subsequence formulation uses increasing indices.

For any integer endpoint pair (u,v), v≠0, with u/v=p_n/q_n, reducedness implies q_n divides v. Writing v=j q_n gives u=j p_n with j a nonzero integer. Thus |vξ−u|=|j||q_nξ−p_n|≥|q_nξ−p_n|. This also covers an integer polynomial normalization even when its endpoint integers have a nontrivial gcd. Polynomial primitiveness and endpoint coprimality must not be identified.

The argument supplies no lower bound for arbitrary sums of different forms, where cancellation could occur, and no result for other approximation families or increasing exponential degree. Divergence of this family is compatible with either rationality or irrationality of ξ=e+π.
