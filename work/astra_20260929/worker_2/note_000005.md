Independent proof of the primitive endpoint-gcd implication.

Use distinct notation (𝒜,ℬ,𝒞) for a primitive integral triple satisfying deg(𝒜,ℬ,𝒞)≤(n,2,n), 𝒜+ℬe^z+𝒞F(z)=O(z^(2n+3)), and the family's endpoint matching, which implies 𝒞(1)≡0 whenever ℬ(1)≡0. Here n≥2 and F(z)=4 arctan(z/(2−z)). Let p>2n+4 and suppose p^d divides both 𝒜(1) and ℬ(1), with d≥1. Work over R=Z/p^d Z; every Taylor coefficient required below has denominator prime to p.

All three polynomials divide by z−1 in R[z]. Write the quotients as a(z), b(z)=b₀+b₁z, c(z), of degree at most n−1,1,n−1. Since z−1 is a unit in R[[z]], their remainder still has order 2n+3. Put c*(t)=t^(n−1)c(1/t).

For ℒ(f)=∫_{−1}^1 f((1+iu)/2)du, direct integration gives F(z)=zℒ((1−zt)^(−1)). Consequently the high Taylor equations, for j=0,…,n+2, are exactly
ℒ(t^j c*(t))+b₀/(n+j)!+b₁/(n+j−1)!=0.
This establishes the quotient equations without assuming a dimension count or normality theorem. Moments used here have degree at most 2n+1; their denominators divide powers of 2 times integers at most 2n+2. The largest factorial argument is also 2n+2.

To prove (b₀,b₁) primitive modulo p, suppose both coefficients vanish modulo p. The first n equations then make c* orthogonal to every polynomial of degree at most n−1. The integral polynomials L_k(t)=2^k i^k P_k(−i(2t−1)), for 0≤k<n, have leading coefficients 2^k binom(2k,k), all p-units. They therefore form a basis over Z_(p). Orthogonality follows by substitution from that of the Legendre polynomials, and their norms are exactly
ℒ(L_k²)=(-1)^k 2^(2k+1)/(2k+1),
also p-units. Expanding c* in this basis forces c*=0 modulo p, hence c=0. The low Taylor equations then force a=0 modulo p. Thus every quotient, and therefore every original polynomial, is zero modulo p, contradicting primitivity of the integral triple. This proves the required unit-coordinate assertion.

Now test the displayed high equations against L_n,L_(n+1),L_(n+2). Their c* terms vanish by orthogonality. Let f_k=2^k/(k!)². From
Σ_r [t^r]L_k(t) x^(k+r)/(k+r)!=f_k x^kH_k(x),
the two functional entries for these three tests are respectively
f_n(H_n,J_n), f_(n+1)(J_(n+1),K_(n+1)), f_(n+2)(K_(n+2),M_(n+2)).
These are obtained by taking derivative orders (0,1), (1,2), and (2,3), respectively, and evaluating at x=1. Thus the matrix M_n in the arithmetic report annihilates the primitive vector (b₀,b₁) modulo p^d. The row factors f_k are units. No inverse of ℒ(L_(n+2)²), whose denominator would involve 2n+5, occurs; that potential cutoff issue is absent.

Over the local ring R, a vector with a unit coordinate extends to an invertible 2×2 matrix U. Taking this vector as the first column makes the first column of M_n U zero. Every 2×2 minor of M_n U is consequently zero in R. Each equals the corresponding minor of M_n times det U, a unit, so p^d divides every minor of M_n. Hence v_p gcd(𝒜(1),ℬ(1))≤v_p Ω_n, with full prime-power depth.

This argument concerns endpoint gcd after full coefficient primitivization. It neither asserts the converse nor computes cancellation between the actual rational numerator and denominator. Division by the approximant endpoint Y still requires Y≠0; no individual-index nonvanishing of Y was used or established here.

The remaining endpoint-gcd dependency is now independently proved from the defining Taylor equations and the displayed Rodrigues identity. The quotient exponential coefficient vector is primitive modulo p, and a primitive kernel vector modulo p^d forces all three maximal minors to vanish modulo p^d. The stated cutoff p>2n+4 is sufficient throughout; no norm for degree n+2 is inverted. This closes the dependency behind v_p gcd(A(1),B(1))≤v_p Ω_n, assuming the family's exact endpoint matching and approximation conditions. It does not identify Ω_n with actual endpoint cancellation or bound the reduced denominator.
