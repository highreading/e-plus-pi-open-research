1. Indices and determinant normalization.
For j≤d and n≥d, n+k+1−j≥1, so the original factorial denominators are defined. Every analytic germ has geometrically bounded Taylor coefficients on some smaller disk, which suffices for absolute convergence against these factorials. The difference formula is exactly Δ_{n,j}(t^rF_n)=Σ_k u_{n,k}P_j(n+k+r+1)/(n+k+r+1)!, with P_j(X)=(X)_{j+1}−(X)_j. No index shift is missing.

For rows indexed by X_i and columns j=0,…,d−1, the highest-degree determinant is det(X_i^{j+1})=(∏X_i)∏_{i<j}(X_j−X_i). Thus the quotient by the stated Vandermonde has degree d and leading homogeneous part ∏X_i, with positive sign. Under the common translation X_i=n+k_i+r_i+1, its leading factor is n^d. This cancels the d extra inverse powers of n coming from the factorial ratios. The surviving scale is (n!)^(−d)n^(−Σr_i), as claimed.

An alternative verification of the limiting sum in (D) uses λ=−c. Absolute convergence permits rowwise summation of det((r_i+k_i)^j). The normalized moments T_j(r;λ)=e^(−λ)Σ_{k≥0}λ^k(r+k)^j/k! are monic polynomials of degree j in r. Therefore the sum equals e^(dλ)det(T_j(r_i;λ))=e^(−dc)Vandermonde(r). This independently checks the constant, including its sign.

2. Uniform remainder and ratios.
The reciprocal-root bound gives |u_{n,k}|n^(−k)≤(2L)^k/k!. After factoring n^(−Σr_i), the remaining determinant is bounded by a fixed polynomial in the k_i+r_i. Summation of the factorial weights gives the claimed polynomial bound in the shifts, uniformly in n. Repeated shifts vanish exactly. The minimum distinct-shift sum is S=d(d−1)/2; permutations of 0,…,d−1 contribute exactly χ_{n,d}E_n. Every other surviving tuple has sum at least S+1. Cauchy bounds and nρ≥2 then give an absolute O(1/n) remainder, with dependence only on the listed fixed bounds. No quantitative convergence assumption on G_{i,n} is needed for this conclusion.

For the ratio, write the two normalized determinants as χ_nE_{Q,n}+R_{Q,n}, with R_{Q,n}=O(1/n). The nonzero limit of χ_n and liminf|E_{V,n}|>0 bound the normalized denominator away from zero. Uniform Cauchy bounds also bound E_{W,n}. Subtracting E_W/E_V gives numerator R_WE_V−R_VE_W over E_V(χ_nE_V+R_V), hence an absolute O(1/n). The amplitudes cancel exactly and need no uniform lower bound individually beyond being nonzero. No lower bound on E_W is required. Replacing either E_{Q,n} or χ_n by its limit with an O(1/n) error would require additional hypotheses; the candidate does not do that.

3. Endpoint application: separate author-derived dependencies, not part of the abstract approval.
Take d=b and common reference U_n=p_{n+1}. Its degree is n+1. The tridiagonal representation used in note_000052.md has Hermitian part (1/2)I, so every root has real part 1/2. Thus U_n(0)≠0 and every reciprocal root has modulus at most 2.

Here is a direct deduction of the microscopic limit from that note's locally uniform convergence q_j=p_j/p_{j+1}→q=−1/(λ_+f). The polynomials are monic and p_0=1, so
U_n(t)/U_n(0)=∏_{j=0}^n q_j(0)/q_j(t),
and consequently
U_n'(0)/U_n(0)=−Σ_{j=0}^n q_j'(0)/q_j(0).
Cauchy's formula and q(0)≠0 give q_j'(0)/q_j(0)→q'(0)/q(0)=√2. Cesàro averaging yields U_n'(0)/(nU_n(0))→−√2. If ζ runs over the n+1 roots, the uniform reciprocal-root bound gives, uniformly for bounded z,
log(U_n(z/n)/U_n(0))=(z/n)U_n'(0)/U_n(0)+O((n+1)|z/n|²).
The logarithm is the branch obtained by summing log(1−z/(nζ)) near zero. Hence U_n(z/n)/U_n(0)→exp(−√2z), locally uniformly. This proves the microscopic hypothesis from those endpoint-specific premises without claiming a convergence rate for the logarithmic derivative.

The other rows are p_{n+1+k}, k=0,…,b−2, and V or W, normalized by their nonzero values at zero. Note_000052.md supplies uniform bounds on a fixed disk and their limits f^k, G_V=2τ/(f−τ²), G_W=2/(f+1), where τ=√2−1. It obtains
E_V=(−√2)^S(−1)^(b−1)/(2τ)^(b−1),
E_W=(−√2)^S(−1)^(b−1)/2^(b−1).
Both limits are nonzero, including b=1. Thus the denominator lower-bound hypothesis follows from these separate analytic derivations. Their independent approval remains pending.
