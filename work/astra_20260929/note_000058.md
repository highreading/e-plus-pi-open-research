These hand calculations suffice to verify the local ideal conclusion: when p>2n+4, if p|h and u is a unit, D_n≡0 forces v≡−(n−1)u, and then E_n≡−(n+1)u² is a unit. When u is a unit, the elimination formulas above reduce it to (D_n,C_n); when u is not a unit, D_n itself is a unit. This argument does not use the actual endpoint theorem that remains unread.
Now prove Ω_n≠0. Suppose instead that all three integer minors vanish. If h=0 and u≠0, D_n=0 forces v=−(n−1)u and hence E_n=−(n+1)u²≠0, a contradiction. If h=u=0, I_12=0 forces v=0, contradicting invertibility of the transition. Thus h≠0; if u=0, D_n=2(n+1)h²≠0, again a contradiction. Elimination therefore gives f_n(u/h)=0. H_n and its derivative have integer coefficients, so the rational root z=u/h of the monic integer polynomial f_n must be an integer.
Rearranging f_n(z)=0 gives n=2−z+(8−6z)/(z²−2). Since z is an integer, z²−2≠0, and z²−2 divides 8−6z and therefore (8−6z)(8+6z)≡−8. Hence z can only be 0, ±1, or ±2. Substituting each back shows that the only possibility satisfying n≥2 is n=14,z=−2.
Excluding this case does not require the original special congruence lemma. The coefficient expansion is H_n(x)=Σ_{k=0}^n (n!/k!)[s^{n−k}]Q(s)^n x^k. For an odd prime p|n, every n!/k! with k<n contains a factor p, whereas the coefficients of Q(s)^n belong to Z_p; the leading coefficient is 1. Thus H_n(x)≡x^n on Z_p[x]. Take n=14,p=7 and differentiate the polynomial congruence to obtain h≡1 and u≡0. However, u=−2h requires 0≡−2, a contradiction. Consequently Ω_n>0 holds for every n≥2.
