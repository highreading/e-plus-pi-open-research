Unreviewed supplement: quantitative reference-polynomial limits.

Define p_0(t)=1, p_1(t)=t-1/2, and
p_(n+1)(t)=(t-1/2)p_n(t)+beta_n p_(n-1)(t),
beta_n=n^2/[4(4n^2-1)], n>=1.
Set q_n=p_n/p_(n+1) and U_n=p_(n+1). The following argument concerns this explicitly defined sequence. Identification with the original endpoint system and its factorial-transform indexing remains an application dependency.

1. Uniform bounds and a quantitative quotient limit.

Let J_m=(1/2)I+iS_m, where S_m is real symmetric tridiagonal with zero diagonal and off-diagonal entries sqrt(beta_1),...,sqrt(beta_(m-1)). Expansion of its characteristic determinant gives p_m(t)=det(tI-J_m). Thus every zero of p_m has real part 1/2. Moreover, q_n is the last diagonal entry of (tI-J_(n+1))^(-1). Normality of J_(n+1) gives
||q_n||_r <= 1/(1/2-r), r<1/2,
where the norm is the supremum on |t|<=r.

Fix r=1/10, so ||q_n||_r<=5/2. The recurrence becomes
q_n=1/(t-1/2+beta_n q_(n-1)).
For d_n=||q_n-q_(n-1)||_r and n>=2, subtraction yields
d_n <= (25/48)d_(n-1)+(125/8)|beta_n-beta_(n-1)|.
Since beta_n decreases to 1/16, its successive differences are summable. Summing this inequality proves that the d_n are summable. Hence q_n converges uniformly to an analytic q satisfying
q(t)(t-1/2+q(t)/16)=1.
The negative values q_n(0) select
q(t)=-1/[lambda_+ f(t)],
lambda_+=(1+sqrt(2))/4,
f(t)=[1-2t+sqrt((1-2t)^2+1)]/(1+sqrt(2)),
with the square root positive at zero. In particular, f(0)=1 and f'(0)=-sqrt(2).

The convergence has a rate. If e_n=||q_n-q||_r, the same subtraction gives
e_n <= (25/48)e_(n-1)+(125/8)|beta_n-1/16|.
Here beta_n-1/16=1/[16(4n^2-1)]. Iteration, splitting the resulting geometric convolution at n/2, proves e_n=O(n^(-2)). Cauchy's formula gives the same rate for every fixed derivative on any smaller disk.

There is also a uniform lower bound. For n>=1,
|1/q_n(t)| <= 3/5+(1/12)(5/2)=97/120
on this disk; q_0 satisfies the stronger reciprocal bound 3/5. Therefore |q_n(t)|>=120/97. Taking ratios and logarithmic derivatives introduces no small denominator.

2. Microscopic reference-polynomial limit.

On a smaller fixed disk define the analytic logarithm
L_j(t)=log(q_j(0)/q_j(t)), L_j(0)=0.
The bounds above and Cauchy's formula imply a uniform bound for L_j'' there. They also give
L_j'(0)=-q_j'(0)/q_j(0)=-sqrt(2)+O(j^(-2))
for j>=1. Consequently,
sum_(j=0)^n L_j'(0)=-sqrt(2)(n+1)+O(1).

The product telescopes exactly:
U_n(t)/U_n(0)=product_(j=0)^n q_j(0)/q_j(t).
For |z|<=R and sufficiently large n, Taylor expansion of each logarithm, with the uniform second-derivative bound, gives
sum_(j=0)^n L_j(z/n)
=(z/n)sum_(j=0)^n L_j'(0)+O_R(1/n)
=-sqrt(2)z+O_R(1/n).
Exponentiating proves
U_n(z/n)/U_n(0)=exp(-sqrt(2)z)(1+O_R(1/n)),
uniformly for |z|<=R. Thus the assertion holds on every fixed compact subset of the complex plane. This proof uses summable errors in logarithmic derivatives, rather than merely assuming that consecutive quotient convergence controls a product of n factors.

3. Coefficient domination and fixed-offset quotients.

Writing the n+1 zeros of U_n as xi_1,...,xi_(n+1), their real parts imply |xi_j|>=1/2. Hence
|[t^j](U_n(t)/U_n(0))| <= binomial(n+1,j)2^j,
0<=j<=n+1.
In particular,
|U_n(z/n)/U_n(0)| <= (1+2|z|/n)^(n+1).
This supplies an explicit coefficient majorant, separately from the microscopic limit.

For each fixed integer k>=0, define
F_(n,k)(t)=[p_(n+1+k)(t)/p_(n+1+k)(0)]/[U_n(t)/U_n(0)].
Its exact product expression is
F_(n,k)(t)=product_(j=n+1)^(n+k) q_j(0)/q_j(t),
with an empty product for k=0. Since q(0)/q(t)=f(t), the quantitative quotient estimate and uniform lower bounds prove
F_(n,k)(t)=f(t)^k+O_k(n^(-2))
uniformly on a fixed smaller disk. Every fixed Taylor coefficient has the same convergence rate.
