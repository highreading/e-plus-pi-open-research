Fix an odd prime p. Put g(k)=k−v_p(k!), J(d)=1+max{k≥0:g(k)<d}, and K(d)=max(1,ceil((p−1)(d−1)/(p−2))). Legendre’s formula gives (p−1)g(k)=(p−2)k+s_p(k). Consequently g(k) tends to infinity, the maximum defining J exists, and g(k)≥1 for k≥1. Thus J(1)=K(1)=1.

For d≥2 write A=p−2 and B=(p−1)(d−1). If k≥K=ceil(B/A), then Ak+s_p(k)>B. Integrality gives g(k)≥d, proving J≤K. This argument does not require monotonicity.

Infinite sharpness: q=p^t satisfies q≡1 modulo p−1 and s_p(q)=1. Therefore d_t=1+(Aq+1)/(p−1) is integral, g(q)=d_t−1, and J(d_t)≥q+1. Independently, K(d_t)=ceil(q+1/A)=q+1, including p=3 and t=0. The upper bound then proves equality for the strictly increasing, unbounded sequence d_t.

Search endpoints: let L be the base-p digit count of K−1, C=(p−1)L, and H=max(0,floor((B−C)/A)). Since K≥2 and C>0, H≤K−1. If H>0, its digit sum is at most C, and AH+C≤B; hence g(H)≤d−1. If H=0, it is also bad. Thus the last bad index lies in [H,K−1]. Moreover ceil(x)−floor(x−y)≤ceil(y)+1 gives K−H≤ceil(C/A)+1; replacing a negative floor by zero only decreases this width. Consequently 0≤K−J≤ceil(C/A), which is O_p(log d).
