1. Polynomial choice and denominator clearing.
Let α∈Z₂ be algebraic over Q. Its minimal polynomial initially lies in Q[X]. Multiplying by a common positive denominator of its finitely many coefficients produces a nonzero P∈Z[X] with P(α)=0. Its degree d is at least one, and A=Σ|a_k|≥1. The polynomial and A are fixed as n varies. Clearing denominators therefore changes only the fixed additive constant log₂ A in the claimed bound. The candidate already assumes integer coefficients, so this step is explanatory rather than a missing hypothesis.

2. Independent valuation derivation.
For a positive integer n, polynomial subtraction gives
P(n)=(n−α)Q_n,
Q_n=Σ_{k=1}^d a_k Σ_{j=0}^{k−1}n^{k−1−j}α^j.
Every summand defining Q_n is in Z₂ because α and n are integral there. If P(n)≠0, both factors are nonzero and
v₂(P(n))=v₂(n−α)+v₂(Q_n)≥v₂(n−α).
Thus the upper bound on approximation depth comes from an upper bound on the valuation of the nonzero ordinary integer P(n). Writing P(n)=2^t u with u an odd nonzero integer gives 2^t≤|P(n)|. Also |P(n)|≤A n^d for n≥1. Hence
v₂(n−α)≤v₂(P(n))≤log₂|P(n)|≤d log₂ n+log₂ A.
The inequality directions are correct. No simple-root assumption, derivative-unit condition, or analytic interpolation theorem is used.

3. Zero evaluations and rational roots.
An arbitrary annihilating polynomial can vanish at an integer other than α. For example, α=1/3 and P(X)=(X−2)(3X−1) give P(2)=0 although 2≠α. The displayed bound is correctly restricted to P(n)≠0; one cannot divide or take log|P(n)| at such an extraneous root. There are at most d distinct integer zeros, so excluding them has no asymptotic cost.

More sharply, choose a scalar multiple of the minimal polynomial. If α is irrational over Q, this polynomial has no rational root, hence no integer zero. If α=a/b∈Z₂ is rational in lowest terms with b>0, then b is odd and one can use P(X)=bX−a. For n≠α,
v₂(n−α)=v₂(bn−a)≤log₂|bn−a|≤log₂ n+log₂(b+|a|).
The only possible positive integer zero is n=α itself. At that single integer the depth is +∞ and the upper bound must indeed be omitted. Rational roots therefore cause no infinite exceptional family.

4. Exact eventual quantifier.
Fix η>0 and K≥0. The quantity
ηn−d log₂ n−K log₂(n+2)−log₂ A
tends to +∞. Choose N beyond both its eventual positivity threshold and every positive integer zero of P. Then every integer n>N satisfies
v₂(n−α)<ηn−K log₂(n+2).
Consequently, linear depth is excluded eventually for all positive integers. The finitely many possible exceptions include small integers and polynomial zeros. This is stronger in clarity, but equivalent in content, to the candidate's stated finiteness conclusion.

For finitely many fixed algebraic roots, take the union of these finite exceptional sets. An unbounded set satisfying the maximum-depth condition is impossible. The phrase 'infinitely many indices' in the final sentence should be understood as infinitely many distinct integer values n; repeated occurrences of one small integer do not imply transcendence.
