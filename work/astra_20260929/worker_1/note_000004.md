1. Quotient equations and factorial indices. Put μ_j=ℒ(t^j). Direct integration gives F(z)=Σ_{j≥0}μ_j z^{j+1}. Suppose an integral primitive solution triple has p^d dividing A(1), B(1)=C(1), with p>2n+4 and d≥1. In R=Z/p^dZ each polynomial is divisible by z−1. Write the quotients as Ã, B̃=b₀+b₁z, C̃, with degree caps n−1,1,n−1. Since z−1 is invertible as a formal series at zero, the quotient remainder still vanishes through degree 2n+2. Set Q(t)=t^{n−1}C̃(1/t). For m=n,…,2n+2 the coefficient equation is
ℒ(t^{m−n}Q)+b₀/m!+b₁/(m−1)!=0.
All these equations make sense in R: exponential factorial indices are at most 2n+2, and the required logarithmic coefficients are p-integral. Thus, defining λ₀(t^j)=1/(n+j)! and λ₁(t^j)=1/(n+j−1)!, we have ℒ(TQ)+b₀λ₀(T)+b₁λ₁(T)=0 for every polynomial T of degree ≤n+2.

If both b entries vanished modulo p, choosing T=L₀,…,L_{n−1} would force Q=0 modulo p. Indeed these polynomials form a basis over F_p and have unit diagonal Gram entries ℒ(L_k²)=(-1)^k2^{2k+1}/(2k+1). The low Taylor equations then force Ã=0 modulo p.
