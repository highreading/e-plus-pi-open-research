## 2. The three-mode endpoint map

Write



$$
C(z)=(1-z)^r(1+z^2)^{2s-1}=\sum_dc_dz^d,\qquad T=2s+1,        \tag{2.1}
$$



where explicitly



$$
c_d=\sum_{\substack{u+2v=d\\0\le u\le r\\0\le v\le2s-1}}
 (-1)^u\binom ru\binom{2s-1}{v}.                              \tag{2.2}
$$



Let $\mathcal E$ be the three-dimensional space of four-periodic
sequences generated by



$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
n\bmod4&0&1&2&3\\ \hline
q_0(n)&1&1&1&1\\
q_c(n)&1&0&-1&0\\
q_s(n)&0&1&0&-1.
\end{array}                                                    \tag{2.3}
$$



Equivalently, these are the endpoint modes $1,i,-i$; the missing mode
is $-1$.  For $q\in\mathcal E$, define



$$
\widehat u_t(q)=
 \sum_{\substack{d\\p\nmid p+r+t+d+1}}
 c_d\,\frac{q(p+r+t+d+1)}{p+r+t+d+1}\pmod p                    \tag{2.4}
$$



and



$$
\Phi(q)=
 \bigl(\widehat u_T(q),\widehat u_{T+1}(q),
                    \widehat u_{T+2}(q)\bigr)^t.               \tag{2.5}
$$



Item 230's Pearson derivation is coefficientwise and linear in the
endpoint numerator.  Therefore, for the phase shift



$$
(\sigma q)(n)=q(n+p),                                        \tag{2.6}
$$



the same proof gives



$$
a\Phi(q)=q(2p),\qquad
 \Phi(\sigma q)=M\Phi(q)+bq(2p)=N\Phi(q).                      \tag{2.7}
$$



The map $\Phi$ is injective.  If $\Phi(q)=0$, (2.7) successively
gives



$$
q(2p)=q(3p)=q(4p)=0.                                         \tag{2.8}
$$



The phase sequence $q(\alpha p)$ has the three distinct characteristic
roots $1,i^p,(-i)^p$.  Three consecutive zero values therefore force
$q=0$.  Since both spaces have dimension three, $\Phi$ is an
isomorphism.

It follows from (2.7) that $N$ is conjugate to $\sigma$.  This proves
the first two assertions in (1.4).  It also gives



$$
O\Phi(q)=\bigl(q(2p),q(3p),q(4p)\bigr)^t,                     \tag{2.9}
$$



so $O$ is invertible.  This is an all-row proof; the finite replay in
Section 7 is not being used to infer invertibility.

## 3. A closed finite-binomial formula

Section 2 yields a coefficient formula that does not require multiplying
the $p$-step recurrence matrices.  Let $\Phi$ also denote the
$3$-by-$3$ matrix whose columns are
$\Phi(q_0),\Phi(q_c),\Phi(q_s)$, and put



$$
\varepsilon=(-1)^{(p-1)/2},\qquad
 S_\varepsilon=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 1&0&0\\
 0&0&\varepsilon\\
 0&-\varepsilon&0
 \end{pmatrix}.                                                \tag{3.1}
$$



Then



$$
N=\Phi S_\varepsilon\Phi^{-1}.                               \tag{3.2}
$$



All entries of $\Phi$ are the explicit sums (2.2)--(2.5).
Let $e_0=(1,0,0)^t$ and



$$
a_0=ae_0=r+2s+2.                                             \tag{3.3}
$$



The inequalities $0<a_0<p$ make $a_0$ a $p$-unit.  Since Item 230
proved that the first column of $M$ is zero,



$$
Ne_0=bae_0=a_0b,\qquad b=\frac{Ne_0}{a_0}.                   \tag{3.4}
$$



Consequently



$$
h_0=\frac{aNe_0}{a_0},\qquad
 h_1=\frac{aN^2e_0}{a_0}.                                    \tag{3.5}
$$



For a denominator-free version, set



$$
D=\det\Phi,\qquad
 R_k=a\Phi S_\varepsilon^k\operatorname {adj}(\Phi)e_0
 \quad(k=1,2).                                                 \tag{3.6}
$$



Both $a_0$ and $D$ are units, by (3.3) and Section 2.  Equations
(1.7) and (3.5)--(3.6) give the exact closed expression



$$
\boxed{\quad
 \mathfrak D=
 \frac{(R_1-R_2)^2+(a_0D-R_1)^2}{(a_0D)^2}\pmod p.\quad}       \tag{3.7}
$$



Thus the numerator in (3.7), built only from finite binomial sums and
$3$-by-$3$ determinants, is an exact equation for the singular
locus.  It is not merely a bounded recurrence scan.

## 4. Feedback polynomial and the $2$-by-$2$ block

From (3.4), $N^4=I$, and (1.3),



$$
h_3=aN^3b=\frac{aN^4e_0}{a_0}=1.                             \tag{4.1}
$$



The characteristic relation



$$
N^3=N^2-N+I                                                  \tag{4.2}
$$



gives



$$
\operatorname {adj}(I-tN)
 =I+t(N-I)+t^2(N^2-N+I).                                     \tag{4.3}
$$



Because $M=N-ba$, the matrix determinant lemma gives



$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(I-tM)
={}&\det(I-tN)+t\,a\operatorname {adj}(I-tN)b\\
={}&1+(h_0-1)t+(1+h_1-h_0)t^2\\
 &\quad +(h_2-h_1+h_0-1)t^3.                                 \tag{4.4}
\end{aligned}
$$



The first column of $M$ is zero, so $\det M=0$.  The cubic
coefficient in (4.4) proves the second identity in (1.5).

In block form,



$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}0&*\\0&B\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 \det(I-tM)=\det(I-tB).                                      \tag{4.5}
$$



Comparing (4.4) with



$$
\det(I-tB)=1-(\operatorname {tr}B)t+(\det B)t^2              \tag{4.6}
$$



proves (1.6).  Finally,



$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(I+M^2)
 &=\det(I+B^2)\\
 &=(1-\det B)^2+(\operatorname {tr}B)^2\\
 &=(h_0-h_1)^2+(1-h_0)^2,                                   \tag{4.7}
\end{aligned}
$$



which proves (1.7).

## 5. Singular factors, ranks, and affine consistency

Because $r$ is even,



$$
p\equiv2s+3\pmod4.                                          \tag{5.1}
$$



Hence $s$ even gives $p\equiv3\pmod4$.  Since $-1$ is then a
nonsquare, (1.7) vanishes only when its two summands vanish separately,
which proves (1.8).  If $s$ is odd, $p\equiv1\pmod4$, and ordinary
difference-of-squares factorization proves (1.9).

There is also an exact rank classification.  Elementary block
elimination gives



$$
\operatorname {rank}(I+M^2)
 =1+\operatorname {rank}(I+B^2).                              \tag{5.2}
$$



Cayley--Hamilton for $B$ gives



$$
I+B^2=(1-\det B)I+(\operatorname {tr}B)B.                    \tag{5.3}
$$



On the singular locus this $2$-by-$2$ matrix is zero exactly when
$\operatorname {tr}B=0$ and $\det B=1$, equivalently
$h_0=h_1=1$.  Otherwise it is a nonzero singular matrix of rank one.
This proves the rank assertions in Section 1.

Singularity itself never contradicts the affine closure equation



$$
(I+M^2)X=4Mb-2b.                                             \tag{5.4}
$$



Indeed, work temporarily over an algebraic closure.  Since $x^2+1$
is separable, a left-null vector of $I+M^2$ is a sum of vectors
$\ell$ satisfying



$$
\ell M=\zeta\ell,\qquad \zeta^2=-1.                          \tag{5.5}
$$



Let $r_\zeta$ be a right $\zeta$-eigenvector of $N$.  Invertibility
of $O$ implies $ar_\zeta\ne0$.  From $M=N-ba$,



$$
\ell(N-\zeta I)=(\ell b)a.                                  \tag{5.6}
$$



Multiplying by $r_\zeta$ gives $\ell b=0$.  Therefore



$$
\ell(4Mb-2b)=(4\zeta-2)\ell b=0.                             \tag{5.7}
$$



The left-null criterion proves that the right side of (5.4) is always
in the image, including every singular row.

## 6. Bottom reciprocity and coefficientwise redundancy

Define



$$
t_q=aN^qv\qquad(0\le q\le3).                                 \tag{6.1}
$$



By definition,



$$
t_0=\Delta_+.                                                \tag{6.2}
$$



There is an equally exact identity at the lower terminal:



$$
t_3=\Delta_-.                                                \tag{6.3}
$$



To prove it, let $q_v=\Phi^{-1}(v)$.  The nonresonant backward pivots
between $T$ and $0$, and then between $0$ and the lower terminal,
are precisely the units audited in Items 223 and 230.  Backward
uniqueness therefore recovers the normalized state $(1,1,-1)$, and
the lower terminal covector evaluates to $q_v(p)$.  On the other hand,
(2.7) gives



$$
t_3=q_v(5p)=q_v(p),                                          \tag{6.4}
$$



because $q_v$ is four-periodic.  This proves (6.3) without assuming
a collision.

The characteristic polynomial of $N$ also gives



$$
t_0-t_1+t_2-t_3=0.                                          \tag{6.5}
$$



Let $x=\mu v$, and define the three terminal residuals



$$
e_0=\mu t_0+4,\qquad
 e_1=\mu t_1-2,\qquad
 e_3=\mu t_3+2.                                               \tag{6.6}
$$



Thus $e_0,e_1,e_3$ respectively measure the $2p$, $3p$, and
bottom/$5p$ terminal equations.  In Item 230's displayed row
normalization, its first three residuals are



$$
e_3,\qquad e_0,\qquad e_1-h_0e_0.                            \tag{6.7}
$$



Hence their simultaneous vanishing is exactly
$e_0=e_1=e_3=0$.

Now let $c$ be the unique vector satisfying



$$
Oc=(1,1,1)^t.                                                \tag{6.8}
$$



Equation (6.5), together with $N^4=I$, gives



$$
O(I+N^2)x
 =\mu(t_1+t_3)(1,1,1)^t
 =(e_1+e_3)(1,1,1)^t.                                       \tag{6.9}
$$



Therefore



$$
(I+N^2)x=c(e_1+e_3).                                        \tag{6.10}
$$



Propagate the two fixed sources $-4,2$ from $x$:



$$
\begin{aligned}
 Z_3&=Mx-4b=Nx-be_0,\\
 Z_4&=MZ_3+2b
     =N^2x-Nbe_0-be_1+h_0be_0.                               \tag{6.11}
\end{aligned}
$$



The residual of Item 230's three antiperiod coordinates is



$$
\boxed{\quad
 R:=(I+M^2)x-(4Mb-2b)
 =(h_0b-Nb)e_0+(c-b)e_1+ce_3.\quad}                           \tag{6.12}
$$



This is the requested coefficientwise identity.  If the three terminal
residuals vanish, every coordinate of $R$ vanishes.  No inverse of
$I+M^2$, and no assumption $\mathfrak D\ne0$, has been used.

Conversely, Item 230's six rows contain the three terminal rows, so
formal solvability of all six implies formal solvability of those three.
Thus (6.12) proves exact equivalence.

The $2p$ right side is $-4$, a unit.  Therefore the three terminal
rows are solvable precisely when $\Delta_+\ne0$ and their two
independent augmented minors vanish.  Items 228 and 230 identify those
minors as



$$
2\Theta,\qquad-\epsilon\Psi,                                 \tag{6.13}
$$



with unit factors.  This proves (1.10).

For $\mathfrak D\ne0$, Item 230 already proved that formal solvability
is equivalent to an original common-log collision.  On
$\mathfrak D=0$, (1.10) remains a necessary formal criterion; this
item does not promote it to sufficiency.
