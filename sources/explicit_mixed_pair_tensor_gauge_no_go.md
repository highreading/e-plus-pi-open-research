# Tensor, exterior, coupling, and rational-gauge audit for the explicit
# $\mathbb G_m\times\mathbb G_a$ mixed system

Date: 2026-08-27 (UTC)

## 1. Question and exact verdict

This note continues
`sources/explicit_low_order_mixed_pair_monodromy_stokes_audit.md`.
Assume, only to test the proposed route, that


$$
({\rm H})\qquad s=e+\pi\in\overline{\mathbb Q}.       \tag{1}
$$


Put


$$
x(z)=e^z,\qquad y(z)=-4\arctan z,
\qquad q(z)=-\frac4{1+z^2}.                         \tag{2}
$$


The normalized fundamental matrix of the elementary mixed system is


$$
\Phi(z)=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&0\\
0&x(z)&0\\
y(z)&0&1
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\Phi(0)=I_3,                                        \tag{3}
$$


and its connection matrix from $0$ to $1$, along the real
segment, is


$$
C=\Phi(1)=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&0\\
0&e&0\\
-\pi&0&1
\end{pmatrix}.                                     \tag{4}
$$



The outcome is a rigorous no-go for every coupling that stays in the
tensor category of this system, and for every rational gauge or split
stabilization of such a coupling.

> **Tensor--gauge no-go.** Let $\mathcal T$ be the rigid tensor
> category generated by (3): it permits finite direct sums, tensor
> products, duals, symmetric and exterior powers, subquotients, and
> algebraic changes of basis. For any resulting presentation that is
> regular at the two endpoints, every entry of its normalized
> fundamental matrix belongs to
> 

$$
> \overline{\mathbb Q}(z)
> [x,x^{-1},y].                                     \tag{5}
>
$$


> Every connection entry at $1$ therefore belongs to
> $\overline{\mathbb Q}(e,\pi)$. Under (1), it belongs to the
> one-variable field $\overline{\mathbb Q}(e)$.
>
> If one then makes a rational gauge transformation which is regular
> and invertible at both endpoints, the field generated by the
> connection entries is unchanged. The same is true after adjoining
> split trivial blocks.
>
> More sharply, within (5), an entry that is an $E$-function cannot
> depend on $y$, and an entry that is a $G$-function cannot depend
> on $x$. Hence every all-$E$ tensor construction forgets the
> $\mathbb G_a$/$\pi$ coordinate, while every all-$G$
> construction forgets the $\mathbb G_m$/$e$ coordinate.

The equality in (1) is the vanishing at the single connection point
$(x,y)=(e,-\pi)$ of


$$
F_s(X,Y)=X-Y-s.                                     \tag{6}
$$


The hypersurface $F_s=0$ is not stable under
$\mathbb G_m\times\mathbb G_a$. It is therefore not a functional
or Tannakian invariant. Tensor and exterior constructions can encode
the vanishing, but cannot turn it into an identity forced by the
differential Galois group.

There is one honest escape from this no-go: construct a genuinely
non-split differential extension whose Picard--Vessiot field is
larger. Such an extension adds new iterated exponential/logarithmic
integrals and new connection constants. No invariance theorem says
that it cannot help. But none of the existing pure $E$-value,
pure period, monodromy-density, or arithmetic connection-constant
theorems supplies the required mixed specialization statement for
such an extension. Producing that extra arithmetic theorem or a
special extension to which a proved theorem applies is exactly the
remaining task.

No conclusion about the arithmetic nature of $e+\pi$ is claimed.

## 2. Picard--Vessiot ring and the connection point

Let $k=\overline{\mathbb Q}$. The system in (3) is


$$
\Phi'=M\Phi,
\qquad
M(z)=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
q(z)&0&0
\end{pmatrix}.                                     \tag{7}
$$


The preceding audit proves that $x=e^z$ and
$y=-4\arctan z$ are algebraically independent over $k(z)$.
Consequently its Picard--Vessiot ring is


$$
R=k(z)[x,x^{-1},y],                                 \tag{8}
$$


and its differential Galois group is


$$
G=\left\{
g(a,b)=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&0\\
0&a&0\\
b&0&1
\end{pmatrix}:
a\in\mathbb G_m,\ b\in\mathbb G_a
\right\}
\simeq\mathbb G_m\times\mathbb G_a.              \tag{9}
$$


The right action on a fundamental matrix is


$$
\Phi\longmapsto\Phi g(a,b),
\qquad
(x,y)\longmapsto(xa,y+b).                          \tag{10}
$$


The coordinate ring of $G$ is


$$
k[G]=k[X,X^{-1},Y].                                 \tag{11}
$$



Evaluation of the normalized connection matrix defines a
$k$-algebra homomorphism


$$
\operatorname {ev}_C:k[X,X^{-1},Y]\longrightarrow\mathbb C,
\qquad X\longmapsto e,\quad Y\longmapsto-\pi.       \tag{12}
$$


Functional differential Galois theory determines the source of (12).
The arithmetic problem asks about its kernel. In particular,


$$
({\rm H})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
X-Y-s\in\ker(\operatorname {ev}_C).                 \tag{13}
$$


A theorem asserting that (12) is injective would prove the algebraic
independence of $e$ and $\pi$, which is much stronger than the
target. The exact weaker statement needed here is merely that its
kernel contains no polynomial $X-Y-s$ with $s\in k$.

This formulation separates two logically different facts:

* algebraic independence of $x(z)$ and $y(z)$ says that (11) is
  the functional coordinate ring;
* algebraic independence, or even the desired linear separation, of
  $e$ and $-\pi$ is an injectivity statement for the numerical
  map (12).

No differential-Galois calculation alone identifies those two maps.

## 3. The target hyperplane is not a group invariant

Under the group law (10),


$$
F_s((X,Y)g(a,b))=aX-Y-b-s.                          \tag{14}
$$


On the hypersurface $Y=X-s$, this becomes


$$
(a-1)X-b.                                          \tag{15}
$$


It vanishes identically in $X$ only for $a=1,b=0$. Thus the
target hypersurface is not stable under any nontrivial right
translation, and in particular is not $G$-stable.

One can also compute all right-translation invariants directly. If
$P\in k[X,X^{-1},Y]$ is fixed by every additive translation
$Y\mapsto Y+b$, then, in characteristic zero, $P$ is independent
of $Y$. If it is also fixed by every scaling
$X\mapsto aX$, only the $X^0$ Laurent term remains. Therefore


$$
k[G]^G=k.                                           \tag{16}
$$



Fixed tensors and differential-Galois identities give equations
stable under the right $G$-action. Equation (15) proves that (13)
is not of that kind. It is an accidental equation at one complex
point of the connection torsor, if (1) holds.

There is an elementary version using the original solution. Under
(1), both


$$
v_s=(1,1,s)^T\in k^3,
\qquad \ell=(0,1,-1)\in(k^3)^*                       \tag{17}
$$


are algebraic, and


$$
\ell\Phi(z)v_s=e^z-s+4\arctan z.                    \tag{18}
$$


The value of (18) at $1$ is zero exactly under (1), but the
function (18) is not zero. The covector $\ell$ is not a horizontal
invariant covector. Hence a tensor contraction can display the
desired zero without giving any reason that the zero is impossible.

## 4. Every tensor construction stays in the same two-coordinate ring

### 4.1 General representation theorem

Let $\rho:G\to\operatorname {GL}(W)$ be any finite-dimensional
algebraic representation defined over $k$. Every matrix entry of
$\rho$ is a regular function on $G$, so it lies in (11). Thus,
in an algebraic basis,


$$
\rho(\Phi(z))_{ij}
=\sum_{r=r_0}^{r_1}\sum_{j=0}^{J}
c_{rj}e^{rz}\bigl(-4\arctan z\bigr)^j,
\qquad c_{rj}\in k.                                \tag{19}
$$


The rigid tensor category generated by the faithful representation
(9) is equivalent to the category of algebraic representations of
$G$ that occur through the usual tensor, dual, subobject, and
quotient operations. In a constant algebraic representation basis,
(19) applies directly. A rational differential-module basis can add
coefficients in $k(z)$, as in (5); provided that basis is regular at
the endpoints, its evaluation still has algebraic coefficients and
the conclusion below is unchanged.

At the connection point,


$$
\rho(C)_{ij}
=\sum_{r,j}c_{rj}e^r(-\pi)^j
\in k(e,\pi).                                       \tag{20}
$$


Under (1), $-\pi=e-s$, so


$$
k(\rho(C)_{ij}:i,j)\subseteq k(e).                  \tag{21}
$$


Taking all tensor constructions cannot enlarge the field. Conversely,
the original faithful representation already contains $e$ and
$-\pi$ as entries, so the aggregate connection field of the whole
tensor category is exactly


$$
k(e,\pi),                                           \tag{22}
$$


and, under (1), exactly $k(e)$.

More generally, every faithful algebraic representation $\rho$ has
this same field. Indeed, a faithful morphism of linear algebraic
groups is a closed immersion. Hence $X,X^{-1},Y$ are regular
functions of the matrix entries of $\rho$ and the inverse of its
determinant. Evaluation at $C$ gives


$$
k(\rho(C)_{ij}:i,j)=k(e,\pi).                       \tag{22a}
$$



This proves a stronger statement than checking individual symmetric
or exterior powers: no algebraic representation of this differential
Galois group can manufacture a third numerical coordinate such as
$\log e$, or any constant outside the original two-period field.

### 4.2 Weight obstruction to an invariant coupling

In the standard representation, the exponential line has
$\mathbb G_m$-weight $1$. The two-dimensional unipotent block
spanned by the constant and logarithmic coordinates has weight
$0$. Therefore


$$
\operatorname {Hom}_G(W_1,W_0)=0                  \tag{23}
$$


whenever $W_1$ is a pure nonzero $\mathbb G_m$-weight and
$W_0$ has weight zero. Indeed, equivariance under
$a\in\mathbb G_m$ would give both $T(av)=aT(v)$ and
$T(av)=T(v)$, forcing $T=0$.

Thus there is no $G$-equivariant identification of the exponential
and logarithmic coordinates. Tensoring can move the logarithmic
unipotent action into a nonzero weight, producing products such as
$e^z\arctan z$, but it cannot turn the numerical additive equality
at $z=1$ into an invariant isomorphism. Any invariant that cancels
the exponential weight also cancels the corresponding power of
$e^z$.

## 5. Exterior powers and the smallest visible coupling

Use the ordered basis


$$
e_0\wedge e_1,\quad e_0\wedge e_2,\quad
e_1\wedge e_2
$$


of $\bigwedge^2 k^3$. Direct calculation gives


$$
\bigwedge{}^2\Phi(z)=
\begin{pmatrix}
x&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
-xy&0&x
\end{pmatrix}.                                     \tag{24}
$$


Consequently,


$$
\bigwedge{}^2 C=
\begin{pmatrix}
e&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
e\pi&0&e
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\bigwedge{}^3\Phi=e^z.                             \tag{25}
$$


Exterior powers therefore produce the product $e\pi$, not a new
comparison invariant. Under (1),


$$
e\pi=e(s-e)=se-e^2\in k(e).                        \tag{26}
$$


Also, $C$ and all of its exterior powers remain invertible. The
coincidence of two components of the single vector
$\Phi(1)v_s$ is not a linear dependence among columns of $C$, so
the usual determinant test is irrelevant.

The smallest system with a visibly off-diagonal exponential/logarithm
coupling is the tensor product of the exponential line with the
two-dimensional logarithmic block. It has


$$
\Psi_\otimes(z)=
\begin{pmatrix}
x&0\\
xy&x
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\Psi_\otimes'=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0\\
q(z)&1
\end{pmatrix}\Psi_\otimes,                         \tag{27}
$$


and


$$
\Psi_\otimes(1)=
\begin{pmatrix}
e&0\\
-e\pi&e
\end{pmatrix}.                                     \tag{28}
$$


This representation is still faithful: dividing its lower-left
entry by its diagonal entry recovers $y$. Its connection field is
still exactly $k(e,\pi)$. The off-diagonal solution
$-4e^z\arctan z$ has logarithmic monodromy at $\pm i$, so it is
not an $E$-function, and has exponential growth at infinity, so it
is not a $G$-function. The minimal visible coupling has therefore
not entered either pure specialization category.

## 6. Exact separation of the pure $E$ and pure $G$ parts

The preceding example is general.

**Theorem 6.1.** Let


$$
f(z)=F(z,x(z),y(z)),
\qquad
F\in k(z)[X,X^{-1},Y].                             \tag{29}
$$



1. If $f$ is single-valued around $z=i$, then $F$ is
   independent of $Y$. In particular, if $f$ is an
   $E$-function, it does not contain the logarithmic coordinate.
2. If $f$ is a $G$-function, then $F$ is independent of
   $X$.

**Proof of part 1.** Positive continuation around $i$ fixes $x$
and sends


$$
y\longmapsto y-4\pi.                               \tag{30}
$$


Single-valuedness gives


$$
F(z,x,y-4\pi)=F(z,x,y).                            \tag{31}
$$


The algebraic independence of $x,y$ over $\mathbb C(z)$ turns
(31) into a Laurent-polynomial identity in independent variables
$X,Y$. A polynomial in $Y$ with a nonzero additive period in
characteristic zero is constant in $Y$: compare its leading
$Y$-coefficient after translation, or take a finite difference.
This proves part 1. Every $E$-function is entire and therefore
single-valued around $i$. $\square$

**Proof of part 2.** Write


$$
F(z,X,Y)=\sum_{r=r_0}^{r_1}X^rR_r(z,Y),             \tag{32}
$$


with nonzero end coefficients. A $G$-function is a solution of a
$G$-operator; the Chudnovsky--Andr\'e regularity theorem implies
that its minimal equation is regular singular at infinity. Hence
every continued branch has at most polynomial-logarithmic growth in
a sector at infinity.

Along the positive real axis,


$$
y(t)=-2\pi+\frac4t+O(t^{-3}).                       \tag{33}
$$


For a nonzero $R_r$, the function
$R_r(t,y(t))$ has a nonzero Laurent leading term in $1/t$.
Indeed, after $t=1/w$, it is meromorphic at $w=0$; if it vanished
identically, it would give an algebraic relation between $y$ and
$z$. If $r_1>0$, the term


$$
e^{r_1t}R_{r_1}(t,y(t))                            \tag{34}
$$


therefore has exponential growth and dominates every lower
$r$-term. This contradicts regular-singular growth, so
$r_1\leq0$.

Along the negative real axis, with $t=-T$,


$$
y(-T)=2\pi-\frac4T+O(T^{-3}).                       \tag{35}
$$


If $r_0<0$, the end term grows as
$e^{|r_0|T}$ times a nonzero Laurent power of $T$, again a
contradiction. Thus $r_0\geq0$. Together with $r_1\leq0$, this
forces $r_0=r_1=0$. $\square$

For matrix coefficients without a rational gauge, Theorem 6.1 gives
the exact identities


$$
\begin{aligned}
\{F(x,y):F\in k[X,X^{-1},Y],\ F(x,y)\text{ is an }E
\text{-function}\}
   &=k[x,x^{-1}],\\
\{F(x,y):F\in k[X,X^{-1},Y],\ F(x,y)\text{ is a }G
\text{-function}\}
   &=k[y].                                          \tag{36}
\end{aligned}
$$


The reverse inclusions hold because finite sums of $e^{rz}$,
$r\in\mathbb Z$, are $E$-functions, and the ring of
$G$-functions contains every polynomial in $\arctan z$.

Consequences:

* an all-$E$ representation in this tensor category factors
  through $\mathbb G_m$ and its connection field is contained in
  $k(e)$;
* an all-$G$ representation factors through $\mathbb G_a$ and
  its connection field is contained in $k(\pi)$;
* any coordinate which actually sees both group factors is genuinely
  mixed and is outside the hypotheses of the pure $E$- and pure
  $G$-value theorems.

This is a special-case theorem for the exact low-order system, not an
appeal to the conjectural global identity
$\mathbf E\cap\mathbf G=\overline{\mathbb Q}$.

## 7. Rational gauges preserve the endpoint period field exactly

The following elementary lemma is the precise gauge no-go.

**Theorem 7.1 (endpoint-field invariance).** Let
$A\in\operatorname {Mat}_n(k(z))$, let $z_0,z_1\in k$ be
ordinary points, and let $\Phi'=A\Phi$ with
$\Phi(z_0)=I_n$. Let


$$
P\in\operatorname {GL}_n(k(z))                     \tag{37}
$$


be regular and invertible at $z_0,z_1$. Under the gauge
$\widetilde Y=PY$, put


$$
\widetilde A=P'P^{-1}+PAP^{-1}.                    \tag{38}
$$


Then the normalized fundamental matrix and connection matrix are


$$
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde\Phi(z)&=P(z)\Phi(z)P(z_0)^{-1},\\
\widetilde C&=P(z_1)C P(z_0)^{-1}.                 \tag{39}
\end{aligned}
$$


In particular,


$$
k(\widetilde C_{ij}:i,j)=k(C_{ij}:i,j).             \tag{40}
$$



**Proof.** Differentiating the first equation of (39) gives (38), and
its value at $z_0$ is the identity. Since both endpoint matrices of
$P$ are invertible over $k$, the second equation gives one field
inclusion. Solving it for


$$
C=P(z_1)^{-1}\widetilde C P(z_0)                   \tag{41}
$$


gives the reverse inclusion. $\square$

The same proof applies after replacing $\Phi$ by
$\Phi\oplus I_r$. Hence adding trivial summands and then using a
rational gauge--which can make the coefficient matrix look fully
coupled--does not add a connection period.

Monodromy is equally unchanged up to algebraic conjugacy. If
$T_\gamma$ is a monodromy matrix in the original normalized basis,
then


$$
\widetilde T_\gamma
=P(z_0)T_\gamma P(z_0)^{-1}.                        \tag{42}
$$


The differential Galois group and formal/Stokes module are invariants
of the underlying differential module, so an isomorphic rational
gauge cannot add an arithmetic comparison.

For an explicit gauge, take


$$
N=E_{12}+E_{23},\qquad P(z)=I_3+zN.                 \tag{43}
$$


It is polynomial with determinant one and $P(0)=I$. The transformed
coefficient matrix has nonzero entries linking all three displayed
coordinates, while


$$
\widetilde C=P(1)C=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&e&0\\
-\pi&e&1\\
-\pi&0&1
\end{pmatrix}.                                     \tag{44}
$$


The original matrix is recovered as $C=P(1)^{-1}\widetilde C$.
The visible off-diagonal coupling has changed no field and no
specialization issue.

Combining Theorems 6.1 and 7.1 gives a gauge-stable purity statement.
Every entry of a rationally gauged tensor construction has the form
(29). If it happens to be an $E$-function, monodromy forces the
$Y$-dependence to vanish. If it happens to be a $G$-function,
regular-singular growth forces every nonzero exponential weight to
vanish. Thus an ingenious rational basis change cannot hide $\pi$
inside an admissible all-$E$ vector, or $e$ inside a pure
all-$G$ vector.

## 8. What counts as a genuinely new coupling

The preceding no-go has an exact boundary. A differential module
trivialized by the same Picard--Vessiot field $k(z)(x,y)$ belongs to
the representation category of $G$; its fundamental entries are
already covered by (19). A coupling outside this category must enlarge
the Picard--Vessiot field.

For example, consider the rank-two extension


$$
\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}'
=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\r(z)&0\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix},
\qquad r(z)\in k(z).                                \tag{45}
$$


A normalized fundamental matrix contains


$$
I_r(z)=\int_0^z r(t)e^t\,dt                         \tag{46}
$$


as its lower-left entry. This extension splits by a rational gauge
precisely when there exists $R\in k(z)$ with


$$
R'(z)+R(z)=r(z);                                    \tag{47}
$$


then $I_r(z)=R(z)e^z-R(0)$. If (47) has no rational solution,
(46) is a genuinely new exponential integral and its value at $1$
is a new connection constant.

More general off-diagonal extensions similarly introduce
variation-of-constants integrals of products of the old fundamental
solutions and rational kernels. This gives two conclusions, one
negative and one positive:

1. A proposed "coupling" should first be tested for a rational
   splitting such as (47). If it splits, Theorem 7.1 proves that it is
   only a repackaging.
2. If it does not split, the present invariant-field no-go no longer
   rules it out. It has genuinely added arithmetic data. But its new
   functions generally retain logarithmic finite monodromy and/or
   exponential irregularity, so they remain mixed. A proof would
   still require a value or connection theorem applicable to that
   particular extension.

There is a trivial all-$E$ interpolation under (1):


$$
\Pi_s(z)=s-e^z,qquad \Pi_s(1)=\pi.                 \tag{48}
$$


It does not help, because


$$
e^z+\Pi_s(z)-s=0                                   \tag{49}
$$


already holds functionally. Beukers' lifting theorem correctly lifts
the numerical relation to (49). Thus replacing the logarithmic
coordinate by an $E$-function whose target value is declared using
(1) discards the very functional independence needed for a
contradiction.

## 9. Why monodromy density and connection theorems stop here

### 9.1 Density is functional, not arithmetic

Positive monodromy around $i$ sends $y$ to $y-4\pi$. Its
integer powers give the infinite set


$$
\{-4n\pi:n\in\mathbb Z\}\subset\mathbb G_a(\mathbb C), \tag{50}
$$


whose Zariski closure is all of $\mathbb G_a$: a nonzero polynomial
cannot vanish on infinitely many distinct points. The exponential
formal torus at infinity supplies $\mathbb G_m$. Thus the local
monodromy/formal data recover the full product (9), exactly as the
Ramis density mechanism predicts.

But over $\mathbb C$, scaling the additive coordinate by
$1/(4\pi)$ changes the monodromy step to $-1$. Zariski density
uses only the fact that the step is nonzero. It retains no arithmetic
comparison between that step and the particular finite connection
entry $e$. Likewise, the exponential torus contains an arbitrary
parameter $a\in\mathbb C^\times$; it does not distinguish the
specific path value $a=e$.

Thus density proves


$$
\dim G=2=\operatorname {trdeg}_{k(z)}k(z)(x,y),      \tag{51}
$$


not the numerical assertion


$$
\operatorname {trdeg}_{k}k(e,\pi)=2.               \tag{52}
$$


Under (1), the left side of (52) is exactly one. There is no
contradiction with (51).

### 9.2 Pure specialization theorems

Beukers' refined Siegel--Shidlovskii theorem applies to vectors of
$E$-functions at algebraic ordinary points. Theorem 6.1 shows that
the all-$E$ part of the present tensor category sees only
$\mathbb G_m$. It recovers the known transcendence of $e$, but
cannot contain the logarithmic coordinate whose value is $-\pi$.

The corresponding pure period and $1$-period theorems can analyze
$\pi$, but do not remember that another number in a proposed
relation is the exponential value $e^1$. Under (1), $e=s-\pi$
would itself be an ordinary $1$-period; that is compatible with the
known fact that $e$ is transcendental. The missing statement is a
comparison between its ordinary-period presentation and its
exponential presentation.

### 9.3 Arithmetic connection and Stokes theorems

Fischler--Rivoal's arithmetic theory of $E$-operators locates
finite connection constants in rings of $E$-values and Stokes
constants in rings generated by $G$-values and Gamma derivatives.
For this example the theorem identifies the already explicit entries
as $e$ and $\pi$. It is a ring-membership theorem, not a lower
bound for (52) and not a nonvanishing theorem for $e+\pi-s$.

The 2026 $E$-period construction similarly places the two kinds of
constants in a common period framework, but supplies no injectivity
of the numerical period map (12).

### 9.4 The conjectural theorem that would work

An exponential-period conjecture, or merely normality/injectivity for
this explicit direct-sum period object, would force (52). In the
language of (12), that is precisely the assertion that the functional
coordinate ring has no unexpected kernel at the connection point.
It is not a consequence of tensor formalism; it is the extra
arithmetic period conjecture.

## 10. Exhaustive no-go statement for the audited operations

Combine the preceding results.

**Theorem 10.1.** Start from the differential module (7), and perform
any finite sequence of the following operations:

1. direct sum, tensor product, dual, symmetric power, exterior power,
   subobject, or quotient;
2. adjoining a split trivial differential module;
3. an algebraic change of basis or a rational gauge regular and
   invertible at $0$ and $1$;
4. selecting algebraic initial vectors and algebraic covectors.

Assume that every final connection matrix being evaluated is
presented in a basis regular at $0$ and $1$.

Then:

* every resulting connection constant belongs to $k(e,\pi)$;
* under (1), every such constant belongs to $k(e)$;
* the field of a faithfully transformed connection matrix remains
  exactly $k(e,\pi)$;
* any resulting coordinate that is an $E$-function is independent
  of the logarithmic variable $y$;
* any resulting coordinate that is a $G$-function is independent
  of the exponential variable $x$;
* the target zero can be encoded as a scalar contraction, but its
  vanishing is not a $G$-stable tensor identity.

Therefore these operations, followed only by existing pure
$E$-specialization, pure $G$/period, differential-Galois, or
monodromy-density theorems, cannot turn (1) into a contradiction. Any
successful extension of this branch must introduce either:

* a genuinely non-split module with a larger Picard--Vessiot field
  and a proved arithmetic theorem controlling its new connection
  constants; or
* a new mixed numerical specialization/period-injectivity theorem,
  even if only for the original matrix (3).

The second alternative can be stated in its weakest exact form:


$$
\operatorname {ev}_C(X-Y-s)\ne0
\qquad\text{for every }s\in\overline{\mathbb Q}.    \tag{53}
$$


Equation (53) is exactly the transcendence of $e+\pi$.

## 13. Final conclusion

The differential module has already exposed every functional symmetry:


$$
G=\mathbb G_m\times\mathbb G_a,
\qquad k[G]=k[X,X^{-1},Y].                          \tag{54}
$$


Tensor and exterior representations merely replace $X,Y$ by
Laurent polynomials in the same two variables. Rational gauges replace
the normalized connection matrix by algebraic endpoint matrices on
the left and right, so they preserve its generated number field
exactly. Pure $E$-coordinates cannot see $Y$, and pure
$G$-coordinates cannot see $X$.

The hypothetical relation is the non-invariant numerical hyperplane


$$
X-Y-s=0\quad\text{at}\quad(X,Y)=(e,-\pi).           \tag{55}
$$


No tensor invariant, exterior determinant, rationally hidden
off-diagonal coupling, monodromy-density theorem, or existing
connection-ring theorem forbids a single complex connection point
from lying on (55). The exact missing ingredient remains numerical
period-map injectivity for this one $\mathbb G_m\times\mathbb G_a$
system, or a genuinely new non-split extension together with an
arithmetic theorem strong enough to control its added period.
