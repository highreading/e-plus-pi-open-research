# Factorial-digit integer combinations: projective saturation and a support-gap theorem

Checked: 2026-08-27 UTC.

## 1. Scope and verdict

Put



$$
\alpha=e+\pi,
$$



and retain the canonical factorial-digit notation



$$
C_n=\lfloor n!e\rfloor+\lfloor n!\pi\rfloor,
 \qquad
 x_n=n!\alpha-C_n
 \quad(n\geq1).
 \tag{1}
$$



If



$$
c_0=4,\qquad c_1=1,\qquad
 c_n=d_n(\pi)+1\quad(n\geq2),
 \tag{2}
$$



then



$$
C_{n+1}=(n+1)C_n+c_{n+1},
 \qquad
 x_{n+1}=(n+1)x_n-c_{n+1},
 \tag{3}
$$



and



$$
1\leq c_n\leq n,
 \qquad
 \frac1{n+1}<x_n<1+\frac1n.
 \tag{4}
$$



This note closes two tempting shortcuts in the prime-support program.

First, unrestricted exact integer combinations of the factorial-digit
forms do not preserve any special arithmetic family.  For every fixed
starting index, already the first two forms have **full projective
saturation**: after endpoint primitization their integer combinations
produce every primitive rational pair $(P,Q)$.  An explicit formula is
given below.  Thus one may force any desired coefficient support by choosing
$(P,Q)$, but the reduced value is then exactly
$Q(e+\pi)-P$.  The factorial gain has canceled, and proving the required
smallness is the original Diophantine approximation problem again.

Second, the untouched truncations have an exact support-dispersion law.  If
$q_n$ and $q_m$ are their reduced denominators, then for $n<m$



$$
m!\mid K_{n,m}q_nq_m,                 \tag{5}
$$



where



$$
0<K_{n,m}<\frac{m!}{n!}\left(1+\frac1n\right).        \tag{6}
$$



Consequently, outside any fixed finite prime set $\mathcal S$,



$$
\boxed{
 (q_n)_{\mathcal S^c}(q_m)_{\mathcal S^c}
 >\frac{n!}{(1+1/n)(m!)_{\mathcal S}}.}                \tag{7}
$$



Two nearby truncations therefore cannot both have concentrated denominator
support.  More sharply, any sequence of truncations satisfying the
denominator-only Subspace-Theorem inequality with exponent $\eta>0$
must be multiplicatively lacunary: its successive indices have lower
asymptotic ratio at least $1+\eta$.

These are all-degree theorems, not finite heuristics.  They do not exclude
an infinite lacunary subsequence with exceptional content, and they do not
prove that $e+\pi$ is irrational or transcendental.  The exact survivor
is stated in Section 8.

## 2. Coefficient vectors and finite differences

Use the coefficient vector



$$
V_n=(C_n,n!)^{\mathsf T}.       \tag{8}
$$



The linear functional



$$
\ell_\alpha(U,V)=V\alpha-U            \tag{9}
$$



satisfies $\ell_\alpha(V_n)=x_n$.  For $a\geq1$ and
$b\geq0$, set



$$
F_{a,b}=\Delta^bV_a=(Z_{a,b},W_{a,b})^{\mathsf T}.    \tag{10}
$$



Thus the primitive endpoint form in the earlier factorial-digit notes is
obtained by dividing $F_{a,b}$ by the content



$$
H_{a,b}=\gcd(Z_{a,b},W_{a,b}).                         \tag{11}
$$



For a block width $B\geq1$, define



$$
g_{a,B}=\gcd(c_{a+1},\ldots,c_{a+B}). \tag{12}
$$



All these digits are positive, so $g_{a,B}\geq1$.

## 3. Exact block-lattice theorem

### Theorem 3.1

For every $a\geq1$ and $B\geq1$,



$$
\begin{aligned}
 \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Z}
   \{V_a,V_{a+1},\ldots,V_{a+B}\}
 &=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Z}
   \{F_{a,0},F_{a,1},\ldots,F_{a,B}\}\\
 &=\boxed{
   \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Z}
   \{V_a,(g_{a,B},0)^{\mathsf T}\}.}                  \tag{13}
\end{aligned}
$$



### Proof

The forward-difference change of basis is



$$
F_{a,b}=\sum_{r=0}^b(-1)^{b-r}\binom brV_{a+r}.       \tag{14}
$$



Its matrix is triangular with diagonal entries one.  It is unimodular,
which proves the first equality in (13).

Equation (3) gives



$$
V_n-nV_{n-1}=(c_n,0)^{\mathsf T}.         \tag{15}
$$



Therefore the left side of (13) contains every horizontal vector
$(c_{a+j},0)$, and hence contains $(g_{a,B},0)$ by Bezout.  Conversely,
iteration of



$$
V_n=nV_{n-1}+(c_n,0)^{\mathsf T}         \tag{16}
$$



puts every $V_{a+j}$ in the lattice generated by $V_a$ and those
horizontal digit vectors.  Their horizontal span is exactly
$g_{a,B}\mathbb Z$.  This proves (13). $\square$

The theorem is independent of the special values of the digits.  It holds
for every integral recurrence (3) with nonzero finite-block digit gcd.

## 4. Smith invariants and full projective saturation

Write



$$
g=g_{a,B},\qquad
 r=\gcd(C_a,a!,g).                                    \tag{17}
$$



A basis matrix for (13) is



$$
M_{a,B}=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 C_a&g\\
 a!&0
 \end{pmatrix}.                                       \tag{18}
$$



The first Smith invariant is the gcd of its entries, namely $r$, and
the product of its Smith invariants is $|\det M_{a,B}|=ga!$.  Hence its
Smith normal form has diagonal invariants



$$
\boxed{r,\quad \frac{ga!}{r}.}       \tag{19}
$$



There is no divisibility issue: since $r\mid g$ and $r\mid a!$, one
has $r^2\mid ga!$.  It follows that



$$
\mathbb Z^2/\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Z}{F_{a,b}:0\leq b\leq B\}
 \simeq
 \mathbb Z/r\mathbb Z\oplus
 \mathbb Z/(ga!/r)\mathbb Z.                          \tag{20}
$$



In particular, $ga!/r$ is the exponent of the quotient.  Therefore,
for **every** integer pair $(P,Q)$,



$$
\frac{ga!}{r}(P,Q)^{\mathsf T}
 \in\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Z}
       \{F_{a,0},\ldots,F_{a,B}\}.                    \tag{21}
$$



If $\gcd(P,Q)=1$, the vector on the left of (21) has endpoint content
exactly $ga!/r$.  Dividing that content produces $(P,Q)$.  Thus



$$
\boxed{
 \text{the endpoint-projective saturation of every block with }B\geq1
 \text{ is all of }\mathbb P^1(\mathbb Q).}            \tag{22}
$$



This conclusion is stronger than merely saying that many support patterns
occur: every primitive support pattern occurs.

The same projective conclusion holds if each $F_{a,b}$ is first divided
by its individual endpoint content $H_{a,b}$.  Those primitive columns
still contain the two independent directions coming from $b=0,1$, so
they generate another rank-two sublattice of $\mathbb Z^2$.  Every
full-rank integer sublattice has finite quotient exponent, and multiplying
an arbitrary primitive pair by that exponent puts it in the lattice.
Thus choosing raw versus individually primitive input forms changes the
finite conductor, not the full projective saturation.

## 5. Explicit universality from only two forms

The saturation has a direct formula which exposes the analytic
cancellation.  Put



$$
c=c_{a+1},\qquad T=a!P-QC_a.                          \tag{23}
$$



The first two finite-difference vectors are



$$
F_{a,0}=(C_a,a!)^{\mathsf T},\qquad
 F_{a,1}=(aC_a+c,aa!)^{\mathsf T}.                     \tag{24}
$$



For every $(P,Q)\in\mathbb Z^2$, direct multiplication gives



$$
\boxed{
 (cQ-aT)F_{a,0}+TF_{a,1}=ca!(P,Q)^{\mathsf T}.}        \tag{25}
$$



Indeed, the second coordinate is $ca!Q$, while the first is



$$
(cQ-aT)C_a+T(aC_a+c)
 =c(QC_a+T)=ca!P.                                      \tag{26}
$$



For a primitive pair, the endpoint gcd in (25) is exactly $ca!$.
Applying (9) to (25) yields the equally exact form identity



$$
(cQ-aT)x_a+T\Delta x_a
 =ca!\bigl(Q\alpha-P\bigr).                           \tag{27}
$$



If



$$
\Lambda=Q\alpha-P,            \tag{28}
$$



then the combination coefficient in (23) is



$$
T=Qx_a-a!\Lambda.                    \tag{29}
$$



Thus choosing the integer combination so that its primitive endpoint has
specified coefficients $(P,Q)$ already encodes the size of the desired
target form in the coefficient $T$.  Boundedness of $x_a$ and
$\Delta x_a$ supplies no new bound after the exact content $ca!$ is
removed.

The determinant says the same thing geometrically:



$$
\det(F_{a,0},F_{a,1})=-ca!.    \tag{30}
$$



The factorial is the lattice index which is then removed by endpoint
primitization; it is not residual approximation gain.

## 6. Consequence for fixed or moving coefficient support

Fix a finite prime set $\mathcal S$.  By (22), one can certainly choose
an integer combination whose reduced $P$ and $Q$ are
$\mathcal S$-units, or have any other prescribed support.  But after
the exact endpoint division its value is simply (28).  The support-weighted
Subspace-Theorem target becomes



$$
|Q\alpha-P|P_{\mathcal S^c}Q_{\mathcal S^c}
 \leq Q^{1-\eta},                                     \tag{31}
$$



which is precisely the original criterion, with no remaining appearance
of $a!$, the factorial digits, or the block width.  In the fully
$\mathcal S$-supported case it is



$$
|Q\alpha-P|\leq Q^{1-\eta}.    \tag{32}
$$



Consequently, unrestricted combinations, determinants, Bezout operations,
or Smith saturation can manufacture the support but cannot manufacture the
required coefficient-relative smallness.  Proving (31) for the manufactured
pairs is exactly the missing Diophantine assertion.  This is why projective
saturation is a barrier rather than a solution.

Restricted finite differences remain meaningful because their coefficients
are fixed binomial numbers and their raw values satisfy the archived bounds.
For those individual forms the unresolved quantities remain the exact
contents and outside cofactors identified in
`padic_subspace_prime_support_transcendence_criterion.md`.

### 6.1 Coefficient boxes and sign constraints do not restore dimension

The same issue invalidates a tempting high-dimensional pigeonhole count.
Fix $a,B$, with $B\geq1$, and write



$$
F_{a,b}=(Z_b,W_b)^{\mathsf T},\qquad
 X_b=\ell_\alpha(F_{a,b})=\Delta^b x_a.               \tag{B1}
$$



For an integer coefficient vector



$$
t=(t_0,\ldots,t_B),            \tag{B2}
$$



the endpoint map is



$$
\Phi(t)=\sum_{b=0}^Bt_bF_{a,b}.                       \tag{B3}
$$



Equation (30) proves that $\Phi$ has rank exactly two.  Put



$$
A_Z=\sum_{b=0}^B|Z_b|,\qquad
 A_W=\sum_{b=0}^B|W_b|.                                \tag{B4}
$$



The full box $|t_b|\leq H$ contains $(2H+1)^{B+1}$ coefficient
vectors, but every image point lies in the integer rectangle



$$
[-HA_Z,HA_Z]\times[-HA_W,HA_W].                       \tag{B5}
$$



Consequently the exact number of distinct endpoint pairs is at most



$$
\boxed{
 (2HA_Z+1)(2HA_W+1)=O_{a,B}(H^2).}                     \tag{B6}
$$



This remains true for a nonnegative box, any orthant, or any other sign
restriction, because those are subsets of the full box.  The apparent
$H^{B+1}$ multiplicity lives in fibers of the rank-two map.  If
$\Phi(t)=\Phi(t')$, then $t-t'$ is an exact integer-kernel relation and



$$
\sum_{b=0}^B(t_b-t'_b)X_b
 =\ell_\alpha(\Phi(t-t'))=0                            \tag{B7}
$$



identically.  Such a collision is a zero coefficient pair, not a new
nonzero approximation to $\alpha$.

Congruence conditions do not change the dimension count.  If one requires



$$
M_P\mid\Phi(t)_1,\qquad M_Q\mid\Phi(t)_2,              \tag{B8}
$$



then the number of possible quotient-coordinate pairs is at most



$$
\left(2\left\lfloor\frac{HA_Z}{M_P}\right\rfloor+1\right)
 \left(2\left\lfloor\frac{HA_W}{M_Q}\right\rfloor+1\right).
                                                               \tag{B9}
$$



In particular, forcing a common fixed-prime divisor $M$ in both raw
coordinates leaves only $O_{a,B}((H/M+1)^2)$ distinct pairs after that
common divisor is removed.  The raw values lie in an interval of length at
most



$$
2H\sum_{b=0}^B|X_b|,           \tag{B10}
$$



and division by $M$ scales that interval and the endpoint coordinates by
the same factor.  Even if the upper bound in (B9) were optimally populated,
an interval-pigeonhole argument would remain at the ordinary inverse-height
scale for a rank-two rational approximation.  With fewer populated pairs it
is weaker.  Therefore coefficient-vector cardinality alone cannot yield an
$H^{-B}$-type gain, nor a power beyond the Dirichlet/Roth boundary.

The same conclusion holds if one prescribes only *large*
$\mathcal S$-parts rather than exact divisors.  For a fixed set of
$s$ primes, an integer of size $O_{a,B}(H)$ has only



$$
O_{\mathcal S,a,B}\bigl((\log(H+2))^s\bigr)            \tag{B11}
$$



possible $\mathcal S$-parts.  Splitting according to the actual
$\mathcal S$-parts of the two coordinates and applying (B9) adds at most
a polylogarithmic factor.  It cannot recover any of the missing
$H^{B-1}$ coefficient-box power.  Endpoint primitization may identify
still more pairs, but cannot increase the projective image dimension.

This is a count-only barrier.  It does not rule out an exceptional
arithmetically selected pair in the box.  Proving that such exceptional
pairs also have the fixed-prime parts required by (31) is exactly the
unresolved support problem.

## 7. A determinant divisibility for the untouched truncations

The case $b=0$ admits an additional all-degree theorem.  Define



$$
h_n=\gcd(C_n,n!),\qquad
 p_n=C_n/h_n,\qquad q_n=n!/h_n.                        \tag{33}
$$



Thus $(p_n,q_n)$ is primitive and



$$
\Lambda_n=q_n\alpha-p_n
 =\frac{x_n}{h_n}=\frac{x_nq_n}{n!}.                  \tag{34}
$$



For $1\leq n<m$, put



$$
R_{n,m}=\frac{m!}{n!},\qquad
 K_{n,m}=C_m-R_{n,m}C_n.                               \tag{35}
$$



Iteration of (3) gives the positive digit sum



$$
K_{n,m}=\sum_{j=n+1}^{m}\frac{m!}{j!}c_j>0.          \tag{36}
$$



Equivalently, (1) gives



$$
x_m=R_{n,m}x_n-K_{n,m}.        \tag{37}
$$



Since $x_m>0$ and (4) holds,



$$
0<K_{n,m}<R_{n,m}x_n
 <\frac{m!}{n!}\left(1+\frac1n\right),                \tag{38}
$$



which proves (6).

Now take the determinant of the two primitive coefficient vectors.  Its
absolute value is the positive integer



$$
\begin{aligned}
 |p_nq_m-p_mq_n|
 &=\frac{n!K_{n,m}}{h_nh_m}\\
 &=\boxed{\frac{K_{n,m}q_nq_m}{m!}}.                  \tag{39}
\end{aligned}
$$



This proves the divisibility (5).

For any fixed finite set $\mathcal S$, take valuations in (5) only at
primes outside $\mathcal S$.  Then



$$
(m!)_{\mathcal S^c}\mid
 (K_{n,m})_{\mathcal S^c}
 (q_n)_{\mathcal S^c}(q_m)_{\mathcal S^c}.            \tag{40}
$$



Consequently,



$$
\begin{aligned}
 (q_n)_{\mathcal S^c}(q_m)_{\mathcal S^c}
 &\geq\frac{(m!)_{\mathcal S^c}}
              {(K_{n,m})_{\mathcal S^c}}\\
 &=\frac{m!}{K_{n,m}}
   \frac{(K_{n,m})_{\mathcal S}}{(m!)_{\mathcal S}}\\
 &>\frac{n!}{(1+1/n)(m!)_{\mathcal S}},              \tag{41}
\end{aligned}
$$



which is (7).

For example, if $m\leq\kappa n$, with fixed $\kappa$, then



$$
\log(m!)_{\mathcal S}
 \leq m\sum_{p\in\mathcal S}\frac{\log p}{p-1}
 =O_{\mathcal S,\kappa}(n).                           \tag{42}
$$



Stirling's formula and (41) therefore give



$$
\log(q_n)_{\mathcal S^c}
 +\log(q_m)_{\mathcal S^c}
 \geq n\log n-O_{\mathcal S,\kappa}(n).              \tag{43}
$$



Thus at least one of two comparable-index denominators carries nearly
square-root-factorial mass outside $\mathcal S$.

There is a particularly simple gap law when both denominators are
$\mathcal S$-units.  Equation (41) then gives



$$
n!<\left(1+\frac1n\right)(m!)_{\mathcal S}.           \tag{44}
$$



If



$$
A_{\mathcal S}=\sum_{p\in\mathcal S}\frac{\log p}{p-1},
 \tag{45}
$$



then, for nonempty $\mathcal S$,



$$
\boxed{
 m>\frac{\log n!-\log(1+1/n)}{A_{\mathcal S}}
 \sim\frac{n\log n}{A_{\mathcal S}}.}                \tag{46}
$$



For $\mathcal S=\varnothing$, two such indices are impossible once
$n\geq2$.

## 8. A harmonic-support criterion and lacunarity

Before imposing a fixed set of primes, the fact that $q_n\mid n!$ gives
a useful ordinary-Roth criterion in terms of the *distinct* denominator
support.  Define



$$
\mathcal A(q)=\sum_{p\mid q}\frac{\log p}{p-1}.         \tag{H1}
$$



Legendre's formula gives



$$
\begin{aligned}
 \log q_n
 &=\sum_{p\mid q_n}v_p(q_n)\log p\\
 &\leq n\sum_{p\mid q_n}\frac{\log p}{p-1}
 =n\mathcal A(q_n).                                    \tag{H2}
\end{aligned}
$$



### Theorem 8.1

If there is a fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and infinitely many $n$ such that



$$
\boxed{
 \mathcal A(q_n)\leq\left(\frac12-\varepsilon\right)\log n,}
                                                               \tag{H3}
$$



then $e+\pi$ is transcendental.

### Proof

Along such a sequence, (H2) and Stirling's formula give



$$
\log q_n\leq\left(\frac12-\varepsilon\right)n\log n,
 \qquad
 \log n!=n\log n+O(n).                                 \tag{H4}
$$



The denominators are unbounded.  Otherwise the convergent fractions
$p_n/q_n=C_n/n!$ would take values in a finite set and force
$\alpha\in\mathbb Q$.  In the rational case, however, the exact
factorial-digit calculation gives $q_n=n!$ eventually, contradicting
(H3)--(H4).

Choose $\delta>0$ sufficiently small in terms of $\varepsilon$.
Equations (4), (34), and (H4) then give, for all sufficiently large
selected $n$,



$$
\left|\alpha-\frac{p_n}{q_n}\right|
 =\frac{x_n}{n!}
 <q_n^{-2-\delta}.                                    \tag{H5}
$$



Roth's theorem excludes (H5) for an algebraic irrational $\alpha$, and
the preceding paragraph excludes rational $\alpha$.  Hence $\alpha$
is transcendental. $\square$

There is a clean friable specialization.  The classical Mertens estimate



$$
\sum_{p\leq y}\frac{\log p}{p-1}=\log y+O(1)           \tag{H6}
$$



shows that, for any fixed $\theta<1/2$,



$$
P^+(q_n)\leq n^\theta          \tag{H7}
$$



at infinitely many indices would prove transcendence.  Here $P^+$ is
the largest prime factor.  More generally, (H3) is the exact
support-weighted target: it permits primes larger than $n^{1/2}$ if the
total harmonic support mass remains below the half-logarithmic threshold.
It automatically controls every prime-power multiplicity because
$q_n\mid n!$.

No estimate resembling (H3) is known for the actual reduced denominators.
Finite scans cannot supply such an asymptotic estimate.  The theorem is
useful because it converts a friability or support theorem directly into a
proof without needing a fixed set of places.

### 8.2 Lacunarity of every fixed-support denominator-only survivor

Suppose that, for fixed $\mathcal S$ and $\eta>0$, a truncation index
$n$ satisfies the denominator-only sufficient inequality



$$
|\Lambda_n|(q_n)_{\mathcal S^c}
             \leq q_n^{-\eta}.                        \tag{47}
$$



Using (4) and (34),



$$
q_n^{1+\eta}(q_n)_{\mathcal S^c}
 <(n+1)n!.                                             \tag{48}
$$



Since $q_n\geq(q_n)_{\mathcal S^c}$, every such index necessarily
satisfies



$$
\boxed{
 (q_n)_{\mathcal S^c}^{\,2+\eta}<(n+1)n!.}            \tag{49}
$$



Now fix any $0<\delta<\eta$.  If two sufficiently large good indices
satisfied



$$
n<m\leq(1+\delta)n,            \tag{50}
$$



then (49) would give



$$
\begin{aligned}
 \log\bigl((q_n)_{\mathcal S^c}(q_m)_{\mathcal S^c}\bigr)
 &<\frac{
  \log((n+1)n!)+\log((m+1)m!)}{2+\eta}\\
 &\leq\frac{2+\delta}{2+\eta}n\log n+O_{\eta,\delta}(n).
                                                               \tag{51}
\end{aligned}
$$



On the other hand, (41)--(42) give



$$
\log\bigl((q_n)_{\mathcal S^c}(q_m)_{\mathcal S^c}\bigr)
 \geq n\log n-O_{\mathcal S,\delta}(n).               \tag{52}
$$



Because $(2+\delta)/(2+\eta)<1$, equations (51)--(52) contradict each
other for all sufficiently large $n$.  Therefore, if
$n_1<n_2<\cdots$ is any infinite sequence satisfying (47),



$$
\boxed{
 \liminf_{j\to\infty}\frac{n_{j+1}}{n_j}\geq1+\eta.} \tag{53}
$$



This eliminates dense intervals, bounded gaps, and congruence windows of
positive relative density as sources of the desired truncations.  It does
not eliminate one exceptional index in each sufficiently separated scale.

The sharp simple survivor is now explicit.  If one could prove that
$q_n$ is an $\mathcal S$-unit for infinitely many $n$, for one fixed
finite $\mathcal S$, then those $q_n$ would be unbounded and



$$
q_n\leq(n!)_{\mathcal S}=\exp(O_{\mathcal S}(n)).     \tag{54}
$$



Equations (4), (34), and Stirling would then give, for every fixed
$\eta>0$,



$$
|\Lambda_n|\leq q_n^{-\eta}   \tag{55}
$$



eventually along that sequence.  The fixed-support theorem in
`padic_subspace_prime_support_transcendence_criterion.md` would prove
that $e+\pi$ is transcendental.  The unboundedness qualification is
automatic: bounded reduced denominators along infinitely many convergent
truncations would make $\alpha$ rational, while in the rational case the
archived exact calculation gives $q_n=n!$ eventually, which cannot remain
supported on a fixed finite $\mathcal S$.

No theorem establishing such an infinite $\mathcal S$-unit subsequence,
or the more general inequality (47), is known.  By (46) or (53), any such
subsequence must be highly lacunary.  This is the exact remaining gap for
the untouched truncations.

## 9. Exact finite certificate

The companion program independently certifies the canonical digits from



$$
\pi=4\bigl(\arctan(1/2)+\arctan(1/3)\bigr),
                                                               \tag{56}
$$



then performs the following exact checks:

1. Hermite normal forms of the value blocks and finite-difference blocks
   agree with the lattice in (13);
2. all two-dimensional Smith invariants agree with (19);
3. the explicit formula (25) and its exact endpoint content hold for a
   deterministic list of primitive pairs;
4. every pair $1\leq n<m\leq100$ satisfies (5), (6), and the
   outside-support inequality (41) for three selected prime sets.

The frozen artifacts are

* `scripts/factorial_digit_combination_support_certificate.py`;
* `results/factorial_digit_combination_support_certificate.json`.

The computations are finite diagnostics and are not used in the proofs of
Theorems 3.1, (19)--(22), or (38)--(53).
