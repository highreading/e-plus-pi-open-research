# The exact normalization bridge between the factorial-Pascal and bordered centered-cosh models

## Primitive denominators, endpoint content, and a finite continued-fraction diagnostic

Checked: 2026-08-27 UTC

## 1. Statement

Put



$$
H(y)=\sec\sqrt y,
 \qquad
 F(x)=\frac1{2\cosh\sqrt x}=\frac12H(-x).
$$



The factorial-Pascal theorem constructs a diagonal Padé pair for $H$
from the signed even-Pascal kernel.  This note identifies exactly how its
natural factorial normalization is related to the primitive ordinary
denominator used in the earlier bordered-determinant theorem.

For $q\ge1$, start with the positive Pascal cofactors



$$
d_i^{\rm raw}=\det\left[
     \binom{2(q+r)}{2j}
   \right]_{\substack{1\le r\le q\\0\le j\le q,\ j\ne i}}
 \qquad(0\le i\le q),
$$



and saturate their common content:



$$
\Delta_q=\gcd_i d_i^{\rm raw},
 \qquad d_i=d_i^{\rm raw}/\Delta_q.
                                                               \tag{1}
$$



Thus $(d_0,\ldots,d_q)$ is the positive primitive integral generator
of the signed Pascal kernel.  Define



$$
e_k=\sum_{i=0}^k(-1)^{k-i}\binom{2k}{2i}d_i
 \qquad(0\le k\le q),                                    \tag{2}
$$



and the factorial-basis Padé pair



$$
p_d(y)=\sum_{i=0}^q\frac{d_i}{(2i)!}y^i,
 \qquad
 B_d(y)=\sum_{k=0}^q\frac{e_k}{(2k)!}y^k.                \tag{3}
$$



Set



$$
g_{B,q}=\gcd_{0\le k\le q}
       \left(e_k\frac{(2q)!}{(2k)!}\right),
 \qquad
 s_q=\frac{(2q)!}{g_{B,q}}.                              \tag{4}
$$



Then $s_q$ is a positive integer, in fact $s_q\mid(2q)!$, and



$$
\boxed{
 Q_q^{\rm ord}(x)=s_qB_d(-x)
 =\sum_{k=0}^q(-1)^k
   \frac{s_qe_k}{(2k)!}x^k}                              \tag{5}
$$



has primitive integral ordinary coefficients.  Up to a global sign,
(5) is exactly the primitive integer denominator vector $c$ in the
bordered Toeplitz construction.

Let $U_j=|E_{2j}|$ and



$$
T_j=\sum_{a=0}^j\binom{2j}{2a}U_aU_{j-a}.
$$



For the primitive $d,e$ in (1)--(2), put



$$
w_m=
 2\sum_{i=0}^q\binom{2m}{2i}d_iU_{m-i}
 -\sum_{k=0}^q\binom{2m}{2k}e_kT_{m-k},                 \tag{6}
$$



and define the factorial-Pascal endpoint content



$$
\begin{split}
 L_q&=(8q+2)(8q+1)w_{4q},\\
 R_q&=w_{4q+1},\\
 G_q^{\rm Pascal}&=\gcd(L_q,R_q).                        \tag{7}
 \end{split}
$$



In the bordered theorem, the primitive ordinary denominator is inserted
in the integral border rows cleared by



$$
\Lambda_q=4(8q+2)!,
$$



giving endpoint integers $u_{4q},u_{4q+1}$ and content



$$
K_q^{\rm border}=\gcd(u_{4q},u_{4q+1}).
$$



The two contents are related by the exact all-parameter identity



$$
\boxed{
 K_q^{\rm border}=s_qG_q^{\rm Pascal}.}                  \tag{8}
$$



More precisely, for a consistent choice of the global Padé sign,



$$
\boxed{
 (u_{4q},u_{4q+1})=(s_qL_q,-s_qR_q).}                   \tag{9}
$$



Changing the cofactor convention multiplies both entries in (9) by
$-1$ and leaves (8) unchanged.  Formula (8) is important when comparing
content data: the factorial-Pascal content and the older bordered content
are not the same integer.

## 2. Why the ordinary denominator is primitive

Let $M=(M_{k,i})_{0\le i,k\le q}$ be the transform in (2):



$$
M_{k,i}=\begin{cases}
 (-1)^{k-i}\binom{2k}{2i},&i\le k,\\
 0,&i>k.
 \end{cases}
$$



This is a lower triangular integer matrix with every diagonal entry equal
to one.  Hence $M\in\operatorname{GL}_{q+1}(\mathbb Z)$.  In
particular, it preserves the ideal generated by the coordinates, so (1)
implies



$$
\gcd(e_0,\ldots,e_q)=\gcd(d_0,\ldots,d_q)=1.            \tag{10}
$$



Choose integers $z_k$ with $\sum_kz_ke_k=1$.  If $g_{B,q}$ is
the gcd in (4), then it divides every



$$
e_k\frac{(2q)!}{(2k)!}.
$$



After multiplying the Bézout identity by $(2q)!$, we obtain



$$
(2q)!=\sum_{k=0}^qz_k(2k)!
 \left(e_k\frac{(2q)!}{(2k)!}\right).
$$



Therefore $g_{B,q}\mid(2q)!$, which proves that $s_q$ is an
integer and $s_q\mid(2q)!$.  The ordinary coefficient vector of
$s_qB_d(y)$ is



$$
\left(
  \frac{e_k(2q)!/(2k)!}{g_{B,q}}
 \right)_{k=0}^q.                                       \tag{11}
$$



Every entry in (11) is integral, and their gcd is one by the definition
of $g_{B,q}$.  Thus $s_qB_d(y)$, and after the harmless sign change
$y=-x$ also $s_qB_d(-x)$, has primitive integral coefficients.

The high Padé equations for $H$, after substituting $y=-x$, are
exactly the high Padé equations for $F$.  Their rational kernel is
one-dimensional.  Consequently the saturated cofactor vector $c$ in
the bordered theorem and the coefficient vector (5) are equal up to one
global sign.  This completes the promised proof that (5), rather than an
unspecified multiple of it, is the primitive ordinary denominator.

## 3. Endpoint scaling

The factorial-Pascal identity is



$$
[y^m]\frac{p_d(y)^2}{B_d(y)}=\frac{w_m}{(2m)!}
 \qquad(m\le4q+1).                                      \tag{12}
$$



With the primitive ordinary denominator (5), take the corresponding
numerator



$$
P_q^{\rm ord}(x)=\frac{s_q}{2}p_d(-x).
$$



Then



$$
\frac{(P_q^{\rm ord}(x))^2}{Q_q^{\rm ord}(x)}
 =\frac{s_q}{4}\frac{p_d(-x)^2}{B_d(-x)},
$$



and hence, writing the coefficient on the left as $a_m$,



$$
a_m=\frac{s_q}{4}(-1)^m\frac{w_m}{(2m)!}.               \tag{13}
$$



The bordered row definition gives $u_m=\Lambda_qa_m$ when the
cofactor sign agrees with (5).  Combining (13) with
$\Lambda_q=4(8q+2)!$ yields



$$
u_m=s_q(-1)^m\frac{(8q+2)!}{(2m)!}w_m.                  \tag{14}
$$



At $m=4q$, the factorial ratio in (14) is
$(8q+2)(8q+1)$; at $m=4q+1$, it is one and the sign is negative.
This proves (9), and taking the gcd proves (8).

Two immediate consequences help keep later arithmetic claims straight:

1. Since $s_q\mid(2q)!$, every prime divisor of $s_q$ is at most
   $2q$.
2. For a prime $p>8q+2$, one has
   $p\mid K_q^{\rm border}$ if and only if
   $p\mid G_q^{\rm Pascal}$.  This is only an equivalence; it does
   not prove that either event is impossible.

## 4. Finite content diagnostic, not an all-parameter theorem

The exact replay uses primitive $d$, rather than the nonsaturated null
vector returned by a computer algebra system.  On the complete finite
grid $1\le q\le30$, it finds that every prime factor of
$G_q^{\rm Pascal}$ is at most $8q+2$.  Moreover,



$$
\boxed{
 G_q^{\rm Pascal}\mid
 \operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,8q+2)^2
 \quad(2\le q\le30).}                                   \tag{15}
$$



The edge $q=1$ is genuinely outside (15):



$$
G_1^{\rm Pascal}=78750=2\,3^2\,5^4\,7,
$$



so it does not divide $\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,10)^2$, although
it does divide the fourth power.  The result JSON gives the complete
factorization and exact lcm valuation check for every row through 30.

Equations (15) and the observed support are **finite diagnostics only**.
There is currently no proof that



$$
G_q^{\rm Pascal}\mid\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,8q+2)^C
$$



for any fixed $C$ and every $q$, nor even a proof that
$G_q^{\rm Pascal}$ is $(8q+2)$-smooth for every $q$.  In
particular, (15) is not used in the proof of (8).

## 5. What the canonical continued fraction does and does not explain

There is a canonical three-term recurrence for these diagonal
denominators, but its initial arithmetic does not explain the smoothness
diagnostic.  Put



$$
J(y)=\frac{2(H(y)-1)}y.
$$



The constant coefficient of $J$ is one, and a denominator of type
$[q/q]$ for $H$ is exactly a denominator of type $[q-1/q]$ for
$J$.  Thus its canonical Jacobi fraction



$$
J(y)=
 \cfrac1{1-a_0y-\cfrac{b_1y^2}
 {1-a_1y-\cfrac{b_2y^2}{1-a_2y-\cdots}}}                \tag{16}
$$



generates the normalized denominators through



$$
Q_0=1,
 \quad Q_1=1-a_0y,
 \quad Q_{n+1}=(1-a_ny)Q_n-b_ny^2Q_{n-1}.                \tag{17}
$$



Normality of the relevant Padé table ensures that these formal
coefficients exist and are nonzero.  The certificate also reconstructs
them from the canonical Stieltjes-type contraction



$$
J(y)=
 \cfrac1{1-\cfrac{\alpha_1y}
 {1-\cfrac{\alpha_2y}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_3y}{\ddots}}}},
$$



where



$$
a_0=\alpha_1,
 \qquad
 a_n=\alpha_{2n}+\alpha_{2n+1},
 \qquad
 b_n=\alpha_{2n-1}\alpha_{2n}.                           \tag{18}
$$



The first coefficients are



$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrrrr}
j&1&2&3&4&5&6&7&8\\ \hline
\alpha_j&
\frac5{12}&
-\frac1{100}&
\frac{313}{6300}&
-\frac{295}{78876}&
\frac{14615}{812548}&
-\frac{14231797}{7398405300}&
\frac{4763505007}{518332959300}&
-\frac{291348958105}{249631237679716}.
\end{array}                                               \tag{19}
$$



For example,



$$
\begin{split}
 a_0&=\frac5{12},& b_1&=-\frac1{240},\\
 a_1&=\frac5{126},& b_2&=-\frac{59}{317520},\\
 a_2&=\frac{1165}{81774},&
 b_3&=-\frac{45469}{1314147120}.
 \end{split}                                              \tag{20}
$$



Already (19) contains the primes $313$, $1109$, and
$19934927$ in reduced numerator or denominator factorizations.  The
canonical recurrence coefficients are therefore not a visible product
of primes bounded linearly by their recurrence index.  The replay checks
(17) against the independent Pascal denominators through $q=8$.

This is deliberately a limited conclusion.  A finite list of complicated
Jacobi coefficients does not prove that no closed formula exists, and it
does not rule out a different continued fraction, a hidden cancellation,
or a direct local-prime identity for the two endpoints.  It only shows
that the standard canonical recurrence does not immediately turn the
finite smoothness observation into an all-parameter proof.

## 6. Replay and logical boundary

The companion files are:

* `scripts/centered_cosh_pascal_border_normalization_certificate.py`;
* `results/centered_cosh_pascal_border_normalization_certificate.json`;
* `results/centered_cosh_pascal_border_normalization_hashes.sha256`.

The replay checks:

1. primitive saturation of the Pascal kernel and the unimodular binomial
   transform;
2. primitive ordinary denominator coefficients and $s_q\mid(2q)!$;
3. the independent ordinary Padé kernel and direct bordered endpoints
   for $1\le q\le12$, including agreement with the frozen bordered
   certificate;
4. the exact content and lcm diagnostics for $1\le q\le30$; and
5. the initial canonical S/J-fraction coefficients and recurrence.

Only (1)--(14) are all-parameter theorems.  The support, lcm-power, and
continued-fraction pattern checks are finite diagnostics.  Nothing in
this note proves that $e+\pi$ is algebraic, irrational, or
transcendental.
